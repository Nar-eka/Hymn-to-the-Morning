## Critical Introduction to "An Hymn to the Morning"
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phillis Wheatley Peters’s poem, “An Hymn to the
Morning,” published in her 1773 book, _Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral_,
depicts a scene where the speaker, a poet, calls upon the nine Muses to bless her with the 
inspiration and eloquence needed to describe the approaching dawn and its transition into 
morning. This moment of artistic euphoria is short lived, as the speaker’s creative 
connection to the natural world is dictated by the passage of time and thus interrupted when 
the increasing intensity of the sun’s rays, touches the speaker’s skin.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;“An Hymn to the Morning” stands out as a unique addition
to Poems on Various Subjects due to its use of imagery that highlights the beauty of nature and its
positive effects on the human constitution. The title suggests a more
religious or biblically reverent tone, similar to that of many of the occasional poems 
present within the collection. Instead, the poem makes use of a much more Romantic style that
is meant to emphasize nature’s beauty and simplicity. Wheatley Peters’s poem follows the 
emerging emphasis of “_individual_ freedom,” a theme commonly explored by many writers within 
English Romanticism (Buzzard LII). This Romantic era literary ideal revolves around the 
“freeing of the individual mind and the individual soul,” it views the [s]ubjective 
experience and the role that it play[s] in the individual’s response to and experience of 
reality” at the forefront of artistic expression (LII). For “An Hymn to the Morning,” the 
speaker is reflecting on the awe-inspiring, tranquil atmosphere that this brief window of 
time is demanding of her. The speaker’s response to her reality is to first call upon the 
nine muses to swiftly provide her with the inspiration needed to appease “bright Aurora [who] 
now demands [the speaker’s] song” (Wheatley line 4). The mention of Aurora refers to the 
goddess of the dawn herself (Carretta 174). Upon receiving the muses’ blessings, the speaker 
goes on to describe the progression of daylight. This begins with the “hail” or greeting, of 
Aurora signified by the ‘death’ of “all the thousand” stars in the morning sky, and as the 
“morn awakes, and wide extends her rays,” a gentle breeze rustles the tree leaves (Wheatley lines 5, 
7). “An Hymn to the Morning,” in its depictions of the simple beauty and pleasures of being in 
nature, also highlights the Romantic era’s focus on what “non-human nature had to offer to 
the individual human soul” instead of how the collective of humanity could shape the 
natural world for itself (Buzzard LIII). Which can be seen further along in the poem, when 
the speaker sitting under the shade of trees and says that the trees display their “verdant 
gloom . . . / [t]o shield [their] poet from the burning day” (lines 11, 12).  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Another aspect of Romantic era literary tradition that Wheatley Peters makes use of in this poem, is the allusion to the
gods and goddesses of Greek mythology. The poem uses these allusions to emphasize the liminality of the moment that the speaker is experiencing. By naming these
mythological figures, the speaker invokes them, their powers and their related domains, which in turn places greater importance on this very brief time of emerging
light. The poem directly names two goddesses, these being Calliope and Aurora, and only alludes to a handful of others. The “ye ever honour’d nine,” called upon in
the first line of the poem, for instance refers to the nine Muses, nine sister goddesses from Greco-Roman religion and mythology (line 1). These nine deities are
“[g]oddesses on whom poets and other creative artists, thinkers, and philosophers depended for their inspiration (March 322). Only one of the nine sisters is ever
named in this poem, that being “Calliope (“Lovely Voice”)” and the goddess of “epic poetry . . . [said to be] the most important Muse because she had the tutelage
of kings” (March 322). Bright Aurora,” mentioned in the last line of the first stanza is the “goddess of dawn.” (Wheatley line 4, March 95). The two goddesses
Calliope and Aurora are called upon directly by the speaker, with the poem emphasizing their importance by italicizing their names. However, the remaining unmade
gods of this poem, Apollo and Zephyrus, are alluded to through their godly attributes. Apollo is the god of music, prophecy and the sun, he is also the leader of
the Muses (March 63, 322). Zephyrus is both “the West Wind and its god. He [is] also the son of [Eos], the Greek name for Aurora” (March 497, 95). Wheatley
Peters’s decides to leave these male deities unnamed, instead alluding to them through their connections to the named goddesses directly involvement in the
speaker’s pleasant experience. This points to her depth of knowledge of Greek myth as well as the importance of the goddesses within this brief moment.




## An Hymn to the Morning.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;ATTEND my lays, ye ever honour'd nine, {% include essay/feature/aside.html text="An invocation of the nine Muses." %}  
Assist my labours, and my strains refine;  
In smoothest numbers pour the notes along,  
For bright _Aurora_{% include essay/feature/aside.html text="Goddess of Dawn." %} now demands my song.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;_Aurora_ hail, and all the thousand dies,  
Which deck{% include essay/feature/aside.html text="deck (v.) 'to clothe in a striking or elegant manner.' Think 'deck the halls'" %} thy progress through the vaulted skies:  
The mourn awakes, and wide extends her rays,  
On ev'ry leaf the gentle zephyr{% include essay/feature/aside.html text="Allusion to Zephyrus, the West Wind and its god." %} plays;  
Harmonious lays the feather'd race resume,  
Dart the bright eye, and shake the painted plume.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ye shady groves, your verdant gloom display  
To shield your poet from the burning day:  
_Calliope_{% include essay/feature/aside.html text="Most important member of the nine Muses. Goddess of epic poetry." %} awake the sacred lyre,  
While thy fair sisters fan the pleasing fire:  
The bow'rs, the gales, the variegated skies  
In all their pleasures in my bosom rise.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;See in the east th' illustrious king of day!{% include essay/feature/aside.html text="Allusion to Apollo, god of the Sun." %}  
His rising radiance drives the shades away-  
But Oh! I feel his fervid beams too strong,  
And scarce begun, concludes th' abortive song.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "An Hymn to the Morning"

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The Romantic era is commonly understood to have 
occurred from the late 1700s to the early 1800s, “[~] 1789 to 1837 was a time of great economic,
social, political and cultural ferment” (Faflak, 2). For Wheatley Peters, living in New England, she
would have encountered the political climate surrounding the American Revolution (1763-1787)
(“American Revolution”). While “An Hymn to the Morning” does not centre themes related to the
political climate of the era. It is important to recognize that Wheatley Peters was living and
writing in a time of war and political change.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Just as Wheatley Peters was inspired by the Romantic era
and its emerging emphasis on the notion of “_individual_ freedom,” that is “the freeing of the
individual mind and the soul” (Buzzard LII). This freedom being the “[s]ubjective experience and
the role that it play[s] in the individual’s response to and experience of reality” (LII). Wheatley
Peters uses the words “An Hymn. . .” to indicate that her poem(s) would be exploring themes
surrounding nature, its influence of people and a person’s individual experiences within it. Other
poets of the era opted to use similar wording to indicate their exploration of these themes.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Romantic literature also emphasises the concept of the Sublime and the Beautiful, through its uses of natural imagery. “An Hymn to
the Morning,” uses its imagery to convey the beauty of nature, while its companion piece, “An Hymn to the Evening,” uses its imagery to convey the sublimity of the natural
world. In comparing the sublime and the beautiful, Edmund Burke states that “sublime objects are vast in their dimensions, beautiful ones comparatively small . . . the
[sublime] ought to be dark and gloomy: beauty should be light and delicate” (Burke 157).
