## _AN HYMN TO THE MORNING_


## Critical Introduction to "To An Hymn to the Morning"

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phillis Wheatley Peters’ poem, “An Hymn to the Morning,” published in her 1773 book, _Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral_, 
depicts a scene where the speaker gains inspiration from nature, after calling upon the nine Muses. The nine Muses being the sister goddesses from Greco-Roman religion 
and mythology, fathered by “Zeus, king of the . . . gods, and Mnemosyne,” goddess of memory (Carretta 173-4). These nine sister goddesses, “Calliope (epic poetry), 
Clio (history), Euterpe (flute-playing), Terpsichore (lyric poetry and dancing, especially choral), Erato (lyric poetry), Melpomene (tragedy), Thalia (comedy), 
Polyhymnia (hymns and pantomime), and Urania (astronomy),” provide inspiration within the “arts, [sciences], and literature,” and are led by 
“Phoebus Apollo, god of the sun . . . music and poetry” (174). This moment of artistic euphoria is short lived, however, as the speaker’s creative connection 
to the natural world is dictated by the passage of time and thus interrupted when the increasing intensity of the sun’s rays, as the morning arrives, touches the 
speaker’s skin.
